# Value and profitability

*(New here? See AGENTS.md at this repository's root for how to use it.)*

## When to use this

Reach for this whenever a pricing or tactical decision risks favouring one side of the value exchange over the other — giving too much value away, or extracting too much — and before treating revenue growth as proof of a good decision.

## Purpose

Marketing has a dual obligation: create value for the market and create value
for the organisation. The two sides must remain aligned. Giving customers too
little value undermines demand; giving away too much value undermines the
organisation’s return.

## How this executes

This framework does not itself state a specific numbered procedure for balancing value — an earlier draft of this section presented a 4-step "Value model" in that shape, but no footnote supports that specific breakdown. What's actually given is the "V" image: value flows toward the company on one side and toward the consumer on the other, and both prongs must stay aligned — too much extraction fails the consumer, too much giveaway fails the company.

**Step type:** Constraint check — both sides of the value exchange (consumer and company) must stay aligned; neither can be sacrificed to the other.

Pricing is different from the other Ps because value returns from the market to
the organisation when customers are willing to pay. Pricing is therefore the
revenue-producing element of the marketing mix.

## Profitability

Revenue is not the same as profit. Profitability must be considered when
evaluating pricing and marketing decisions. A decision that increases volume or
revenue can still reduce the value retained by the organisation.

The source names gross margin and other profitability levers elsewhere, but no
single profitability formula is established in this Framework. Use the
organisation’s verified financial definitions for calculations.

## Distribution and value

Distribution also affects value and profitability. Channel arrangements can
create physical availability and reach, but they also determine how value and
margin are shared through the route to market.

## Supporting Principles

### Value is marketing’s dual obligation

Marketing must generate value for both customers and the organisation. The two
requirements are simultaneous, not alternative priorities.

### Pricing is the revenue-producing P

Pricing returns value from the market and produces revenue, while the other
marketing-mix elements primarily incur costs in delivering the offer.

### Profit over revenue

Profit should be examined rather than assuming revenue is the complete measure
of commercial success.

## Boundaries and failure modes

- Do not define customer value only as the organisation’s revenue.
- Do not define organisational value only as market share or volume.
- Do not treat price as the whole of pricing.
- Do not use revenue growth as proof of improved profitability.
- Do not ignore channel economics when assessing distribution decisions.
- Do not invent a margin or profit formula where the financial inputs are not
  available.

## Embedded Playbooks

### Van Westendorp Price-Setting Procedure

This ordered process is embedded here because it is self-contained within the originating Framework.

**Inputs and preconditions:** None explicit beyond having rejected 'finger in the wind' pricing.

**Steps:**

1. **Establish real price-sensitivity data across the four reference points.** *(Adapted for agent execution — states the evidence standard, not a transcript claim.)* Obtain real customer price-sensitivity responses across the four reference points — price too cheap relative to perceived value, price a bargain relative to perceived value, price expensive relative to perceived value, price too expensive relative to perceived value — for the relevant population, from existing pricing research, prior studies, or any genuine source that actually captures it, not a plausible-sounding number. If no such evidence exists, that gap must be stated honestly before the remaining steps run.
   **Step type:** Data-gathering — real customer price-sensitivity evidence, never invented.
2. **Accumulate answers across the whole sample.** we're then able to take those numbers and accumulate them together for the whole market.
   **Step type:** Decision (criteria-guided) — a deterministic aggregation once responses are gathered.
3. **Plot all four question curves.** We can draw it for all the four questions... The other two Van Vestendorp questions build in the other direction.
   **Step type:** Decision (criteria-guided).
4. **Find the intersection ('magic' price zone).** the point of the Van Vestendorp analysis is to find that magical golden space.
   **Step type:** Decision (criteria-guided) — a deterministic computation once the four curves are plotted.
5. **Select the price.** we can put it as far across as we can to get the best value for money, which in our case was £1,500.
   **Step type:** Evidence-and-judgement — selecting a specific point within the identified zone.

**Output:** A specific selected price point (worked example: a selected price of £1,500).

**Boundary:** Do not add steps, thresholds, or prerequisites that the source does not specify.
