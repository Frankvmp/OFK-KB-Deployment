# Positioning and brand codes

## When to use this

*(Process framing — not a sourced claim; no footnote.)* Reach for this once a position exists and needs to be made recognisable and consistent — selecting and applying the visual/sensory codes that carry that position across every touchpoint, or checking a proposed position with the Three Cs test before it directs execution.

## Purpose

Positioning defines the meaning a brand should hold for a chosen target in
relation to alternatives. Brand codes make that position recognisable and
consistent across the points where people encounter the brand. [^distinction]
[^codes]

## Two dimensions

- **Distinctiveness** makes the brand recognisable as itself. It is created by
  identifiable codes such as a logo, colour, typography, packaging, sound,
  characters, or other sensory assets.
- **Differentiation** gives the brand a meaningful reason to be preferred over
  alternatives. It is derived from the brand’s position and the value that
  position offers the target.

Distinctiveness and differentiation are related but not interchangeable. A
brand may be easy to recognise without offering a compelling difference, or
meaningfully different without being recognisable. [^distinction]

## Distinctive asset assessment

An asset grid can assess each code on two dimensions: the percentage of people
who link the code to any brand, and the percentage of those links that return to
the specific brand. The first is the fame component; the second is the
brand-specific association component. [^codes]

**Invokes:** Distinctive Asset Grid (Metric). *(not yet drafted)*; [Brand Palette](../schemas/brand-palette-selected-small-code-set.md) (Schema, the output of applying this assessment).

## Positioning test

Test a proposed position through three questions:

1. **Consumer:** is it meaningful and relevant to the target?
2. **Competitor:** is it distinctive relative to alternatives?
3. **Company:** can the organisation deliver it consistently?

A position that fails any of these tests is not ready to direct execution.
[^three-cs]

**Invokes:** [Three Cs](../playbooks/three-cs-positioning-test-consumer-competitor-company.md) (Playbook, the full test procedure).

## Supporting Principle: positioning drives touchpoints

The chosen position should be expressed through the touchpoints where people
experience the brand. Touchpoint improvement is not only a generic service task;
each touchpoint should also deliver the intended meaning consistently.
[^touchpoints]

## Supporting Principle: codification

Once a palette has been selected, communications should use it consistently.
Applying the codes across every relevant execution improves the chance that
communications will be recalled and linked to the brand. [^codification]

## Supporting Case Studies

### KitKat

KitKat illustrates the relationship between distinctive codes and brand meaning:
recognisable assets can carry the brand belief across executions. The specific
assets and claim must be drafted from the cited passage before promotion to
`verified`.

### Sephora

Sephora illustrates a selected brand palette rather than an unlimited collection
of identifiers. The palette is a compact set intended to make the brand
recognisable across applications.

## Supporting Metrics

The distinctive asset grid provides the usable measurement structure: fame and
brand-specific association for each code. The source does not specify a single
formula beyond comparing those two percentages. [^codes]

## Boundaries and failure modes

- Do not treat positioning as a slogan or copy line.
- Do not confuse recognisability with meaningful differentiation.
- Do not select codes without checking both fame and brand-specific association.
- Do not use an unlimited palette when disciplined consistency is required.
- Do not claim that a position is valid without consumer, competitor, and
  company tests. [^three-cs]
- Do not promise a position the organisation cannot deliver.
- Do not invent gold, silver, or bronze thresholds when the source does not
  specify them.
