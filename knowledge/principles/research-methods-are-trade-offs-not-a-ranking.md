# Research methods are trade-offs, not a ranking

## When to use this

Reach for this Principle when choosing a research method, comparing proposals, or interpreting evidence from one method as if it were complete. It helps an agent ask what each method can and cannot establish before recommending it.

## Principle

Research methods are not a universal best-to-worst ranking. Each method offers useful capabilities and carries limitations, so selection must follow the evidence need rather than a fixed hierarchy. [^method-tradeoffs]

Secondary data offers speed and low cost; surveys offer quantitative breadth; focus groups offer rapid qualitative exploration; ethnography offers contextual depth; and conjoint analysis derives choice drivers from trade-offs. The source presents these as different forms of illumination, not interchangeable substitutes. [^method-summary]

## Why it matters

A method can be executed correctly and still be wrong for the question. Treating one method as universally superior hides its boundaries and can produce precise but incomplete evidence. [^method-tradeoffs]

The practical response is to combine methods where their strengths complement one another, while keeping each method's evidential limits visible.
