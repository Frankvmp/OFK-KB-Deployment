# Discount effects must be judged by profit and source of sales

## When to use this

Reach for this Principle when evaluating a price promotion, interpreting a promotional sales spike, or estimating whether a discount created genuinely additional demand.

## Principle

The percentage reduction in price does not translate into an equal percentage reduction in profit. When costs remain largely unchanged, a discount can remove a much larger share of profit and require substantial additional volume merely to restore the original result. [^profit-impact]

A promotional sales uplift is not wholly incremental. Sales may come from extra trips, stock-up, or competitor switching, but they may also cannibalise the organisation's other products, pull future purchases forward, or subsidise customers who would have bought at full price. [^sales-source]

## Why it matters

Counting the whole sales spike as success hides both margin loss and the quality of the demand created. The correct evaluation examines profit and separates beneficial incremental sales from displaced or subsidised sales.

## Related distinctions

More units sold is not the same as more value retained. A promotion can increase volume while weakening both current profit and future full-price demand.

## Boundaries and failure modes

- Do not apply the source's worked-example percentages without the organisation's own prices and costs.
- Do not count displaced or subsidised purchases as wholly incremental.
- Do not evaluate a promotion only through revenue or unit sales.
- Do not assume additional volume will restore the lost margin without calculation.
