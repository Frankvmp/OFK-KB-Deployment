# Triangulate secondary data without assuming it is correct

## When to use this

Reach for this Principle at the start of a research task, when existing reports, internal archives, search results, or AI-organised secondary material are available. Use it to balance efficient reuse with explicit source checks.

## Principle

Secondary research should draw on multiple reports where possible. Comparing sources can improve confidence through triangulation, but it does not guarantee accuracy. [^secondary-triangulation]

Because secondary data was created by someone else for another purpose, it may be outdated, unreliable, incomplete, or impossible to explore below the published report. Those limitations must remain attached to any conclusion derived from it. [^secondary-limits]

## Why it matters

Existing information is often fast and inexpensive to obtain, so it is the correct starting point. Its convenience can also make weak evidence appear settled. Triangulation strengthens the starting evidence without removing the need to inspect provenance and gaps. [^secondary-triangulation] [^secondary-limits]

## Related distinctions

This Principle concerns how to evaluate secondary evidence after finding it. The [Market Research Method Sequence](../playbooks/market-research-method-sequence-secondary-qualitative-quantitative-mid-stage-focus-groups.md) establishes that secondary data should be checked before generating primary research.

Triangulation can increase confidence, but the source presents it as a probable improvement in accuracy rather than a guarantee. The reliability, timeliness, completeness, and depth limits still apply. [^secondary-triangulation] [^secondary-limits]

## Boundaries and failure modes

- Do not assume that multiple sources are independent or equally reliable.
- Do not treat older or partial secondary evidence as current or complete. [^secondary-limits]
- Do not invent missing detail when the underlying data cannot be explored further. [^secondary-limits]
