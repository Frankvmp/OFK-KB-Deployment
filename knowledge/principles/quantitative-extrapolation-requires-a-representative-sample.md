# Quantitative extrapolation requires a representative sample

## When to use this

Reach for this Principle before generalising survey results to a market, comparing segments, estimating market size, or presenting a percentage as population evidence.

## Principle

A sample must be representative in two ways: it must include the relevant types of consumer in the market, and it must be numerically robust enough to support extrapolation to the intended population. [^representative-sample]

Only on that basis can sample proportions be treated as estimates of the wider market, used for market sizing, divided into groups, or compared over time. [^survey-extrapolation]

## Why it matters

A precise percentage does not establish representativeness. The result inherits the limits of who was sampled and whether the sample can support the intended extrapolation.

This Principle stops an agent from converting a convenient respondent count into a market-wide claim without checking composition, population, confidence level, and margin of error.

## Related distinctions

Representativeness is the condition for generalisation. The embedded [Sample Size Calculator Procedure](../frameworks/research-informs-understanding-not-strategy.md#sample-size-calculator-procedure) calculates the numerical sample requirement, but sample size alone does not prove that relevant consumer types were included. [^representative-sample]

The [Three-Section Questionnaire Build Order](../playbooks/three-section-questionnaire-build-order-demographic-attitudinal-behavioural.md) structures what is asked after the sample requirement is established.

## Boundaries and failure modes

- Do not generalise from an unrepresentative qualitative group.
- Do not treat a large sample as representative if important consumer types are absent. [^representative-sample]
- Do not present sample percentages as exact population facts; retain the declared confidence and error limits. [^survey-uncertainty]
